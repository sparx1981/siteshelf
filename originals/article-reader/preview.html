Why we stopped measuring everything

A small design team deleted its dashboards for a quarter. Here is what they noticed, what they missed, and what they kept.

HEHannah Ellis12 October 2026 · 11 min read
On this page

On this page

    The dashboard on the wall

    For three years our team kept a dashboard on the wall. It showed sign-ups, task completion, page speed and a dozen other numbers that changed colour when they drifted. Nobody remembered who asked for it first, and nobody had ever turned it off.

    The trouble was not that the numbers were wrong. They were almost always right. The trouble was that we had begun to treat movement in a number as if it were a decision, and decisions as if they were something that happened to us.

    What we did instead

    In January we switched the screen off. We kept a single weekly conversation instead, forty minutes, with one rule: nobody could cite a metric unless they could also say what they would do differently because of it.

    A number you cannot act on is a mood, not a measurement.

    The first two weeks were uncomfortable. People reached for facts and found they were holding only impressions. By the third week the meetings had changed shape: shorter, more specific, and oddly warmer.

    What we lost

    We lost early warning on two things. A slow rise in checkout errors went unnoticed for nine days, and a pricing page change quietly cost us conversions we could have recovered. Both mattered. Neither was fatal.

    What we gained

    Decisions sped up because they no longer waited for a chart to agree with us. We also found that people were far more willing to say "I do not know" when there was no screen implying they should.

    • Weekly meetings dropped from sixty minutes to forty.
    • Experiments were proposed with a stated next step.
    • Fewer metrics were argued about, more were chosen.

    What we kept

    By April we had brought back exactly three numbers, each with an owner and a written action attached. The wall screen stayed off. If you are tempted to try this yourself, start smaller: turn off one dashboard for one month and write down what you reach for instead.

    1. The dashboard cost roughly one engineer-week per quarter to keep running.
    2. We borrowed the "what would you do differently" rule from a retired hospital operations manager.
    3. This was an informal observation, not a controlled study. Treat it as a story, not evidence.
