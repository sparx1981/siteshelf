The Quiet Return of the Village Orchard

Across the valley, forgotten apple trees are being pruned, named and picked again — and a whole food culture is coming with them.

    The tree behind the barn

    Marguerite Oakes found the first tree by accident, while looking for a lost goat. It was leaning against the back wall of a collapsed barn, its trunk twisted like a wrung cloth, and it was carrying more apples than anything that old had a right to.

    That was six years ago. Today she keeps a notebook of ninety-four trees within a morning’s walk of her door, each one numbered, photographed and, where possible, named.

    A map made of fruit

    The valley once held hundreds of orchards, most no bigger than a back garden. Varieties were local by necessity: an apple that ripened in the wet August of the north slope would sulk in the dry south.Surviving records list 212 named local varieties in 1912. The county’s fruit group has so far matched forty-one.

    “An orchard is the slowest conversation you’ll ever have with a place.”

    Volunteers now spend winters grafting cuttings from the oldest survivors, passing them to anyone with a patch of ground and some patience. The offspring are clones, which means the old trees are, in a sense, still alive.

    What the fruit remembers

    Taste is the last archive. A russet that cracks with a nutty sweetness, a cooking apple that collapses into snow: these flavours are carried in nothing but the trees. Lose the tree, and the flavour goes with it.

    “We kept the recipes, but forgot the ingredients,” says cider maker Tomás Harlow. “Now the ingredients are coming back.”

    Making room for slow things

    Not everyone is convinced. Orchards need decades, and decades are a hard sell to a landowner paying rent. But a modest fund, paid out per surviving tree, has begun to shift the arithmetic.

    The unexpected beneficiaries are bees. In the first spring after pruning, the volunteers counted four times as many pollinators as in the year before.

    The long harvest

    On the last Saturday of October the valley holds its pressing day. A hand-cranked press stands in the square, children carry buckets, and a table of unlabelled juices waits for a verdict.

    Marguerite stands at the edge of it all with her notebook. Tree number ninety-five, she says, is a promising one.
