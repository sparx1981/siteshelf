The Lamp at Skerrow Point

Every winter, someone lights the window of an empty lighthouse cottage. Nobody will say who.

By Ines Callow · 6 min read

The lighthouse at Skerrow Point was automated in 1987, and the keeper's cottage has stood empty ever since. Yet every winter, someone leaves a lamp burning in its upstairs window.

Nobody on the island will say who. The postmistress shrugs, the fishermen change the subject, and the children — who know everything — invent a different answer each year.

A habit, not a mystery

I spent the winter of last year trying to find out. What I found was less a mystery than a habit: a community that had decided, without ever deciding, that the window should not go dark.

“It isn't for the ships. It's for us.”— a retired net-mender

The pattern is older than the lighthouse. Parish records mention a lamp at Skerrow as early as 1790, kept by whoever had lost someone to the water that year. It was a private grief made public by a single flame.

Forty-one names

In 1962 the practice nearly ended. The cottage roof failed, and the council proposed demolition. A petition of forty-one names — nearly the whole island — saved it, and the signatories each took a month of the year to look after the lamp.

Today the rota lives on a faded sheet taped inside the harbour shop door. There are twelve names and twelve months, and the names change quietly, the way old things are passed on: no ceremony, just a different hand on the switch.
