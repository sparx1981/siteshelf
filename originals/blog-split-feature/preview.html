What the Salt Marsh Remembers

When a sea wall gave way, a farm became a shoreline, and a scientist with a stake in her hand began to count the mud.

By Tomas Verrill14 min read · October 2026

    0%

      OneA wall gives way

      The sea wall at Tern Reach failed on a Tuesday in January, quietly, the way old things do. There was no roar and no wall of water, only a seam of brown tide finding a gap the width of a farm gate and then, by evening, the width of a lorry. By Friday there was a new shoreline, and eleven hectares of pasture that had been dry since 1962 were learning to be wet again.

      Note 1. Tern Reach lies on the invented Halmer Estuary; tide ranges there reach 5.8 metres.

      The farmers who had worked the land stood on the bank with their hands in their pockets. Nobody blamed the sea. The wall had been patched every spring for as long as anyone remembered, and every spring the patches had cost a little more than the field was worth.

      TwoThe first winter

      Ines Calloway arrived the following week with a borrowed boat, a tide table and a rucksack of wooden stakes. She is an ecologist who measures mud for a living, which sounds like a joke until you watch her do it. Each stake went into the ground at a fixed height, and each fortnight she returned to see how much the water had left behind.

      Note 2. Calloway’s survey method uses fixed marker stakes, read every fourteen days across a full tidal year.

      The first winter gave her almost nothing. Storms took more than they brought, and the new creek cut itself a channel so straight that she worried it would never learn to meander. “A marsh is not a hole in the ground,” she told me, standing in wellingtons the colour of the water. “It is an argument between the tide and the land, and arguments need time.”

      “We did not plant the marsh. We stopped standing in its way.”
      Ines Calloway, marsh ecologist

      ThreeMud, slowly

      Mud, it turns out, is a kind of patience. On a spring tide the estuary carries a fine silt that settles in the slack water just before the ebb, a few grains at a time, so that a good year can raise the marsh by the thickness of a coin. The stakes recorded it: two millimetres, then four, then a summer when nothing moved at all.

      Note 3. Typical estuary accretion is a few millimetres a year; the first summers at Tern Reach averaged 3.1.

      What changed the arithmetic was the first plant. Glasswort arrived by itself, green fingers in the bare silt, slowing the water around each stem so that more silt fell. The marsh, like most good ideas, began to feed itself.

      FourLavender

      By the second summer the upper marsh had turned the colour of weak tea and then, quite suddenly, lilac. Sea lavender had come in on the tide line, a haze of small flowers over grey leaves, and behind it came the insects that follow flowers anywhere. Redshank nested in the tussocks, and on the evening I visited a heron stood in a creek so still that it seemed to be part of the plan.

      Note 4. Sea lavender (Limonium) is a salt-tolerant perennial of the upper marsh and a late-season nectar source.

      The local fishers, who had expected a ruined field, started to bring their children. Bass and mullet were using the creeks as a nursery, and the older men said they had not seen the shallows so crowded since they were boys.

      FiveWhat stays

      Nothing about the marsh is finished, and that is the point of it. It will rise with the sea or it will drown, and no one at Tern Reach pretends to know which. What they have is a better bet than a wall: ground that grows, a coast that absorbs, a place that gets more useful each year it is left alone.

      Note 5. Managed realignment, letting the sea back in on purpose, is increasingly used to build shoreline resilience.

      On my last morning the tide came in across the flats as it always does, one thin sheet after another, and the light went from pewter to pink. Calloway checked her stakes, wrote down the numbers, and looked up. “It remembers,” she said, meaning the mud. Then she went to find her boat.
