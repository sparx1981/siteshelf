The Last Lighthouse Keepers of Brittany

Automation ended the profession decades ago. A handful of people on the Atlantic coast never quite got the message.

By Élodie MarchettiPhotographs by Jan Roux11 Oct 2026
In this storyThe night shiftWhat the light is forA town that remembersThe last winter

At a quarter to five in the afternoon, Yannick Le Goff climbs three hundred and twelve steps to the top of a granite tower he has never owned and will never leave. The wind is already pulling at the rail. Below him the sea is the colour of slate, and somewhere beyond the reef a fishing boat is turning for home.

He does not need to be here. The lamp was automated in 1991, and a small box in a cupboard now decides when it turns on. But every evening Yannick climbs anyway, checks the lens, wipes the salt from the brass, and writes a line in a logbook that nobody has asked to read in thirty years.

The night shift

The job once had a rhythm. Keepers worked in threes, four weeks on the rock and two weeks ashore, and the station ran on routine: oil, wicks, polish, weather. The routine was the point. A lighthouse is only useful if it never fails, and nothing never fails without people who care about it more than is strictly sensible.

Yannick joined at nineteen, the youngest keeper on the coast by a decade. He remembers the first storm, the way the whole tower hummed like a struck bell, and the older man who handed him a mug of coffee and said only, “Now you know what it is for.”

“A light is a promise you make to strangers.”

What the light is for

It is easy to say that satellites made the lighthouse obsolete. Every ship carries a screen that knows its position within a metre. But screens fail, batteries die and signals drift, and there are sailors on this coast who still look for a beam on the horizon before they trust anything else.

“The light is not for the ships that are fine,” Yannick says. “It is for the one that is not.” He tells the story of a winter night in 2009 when the automatic system froze and a trawler with a broken engine steered by his hand-lit lamp for six hours until the tugs arrived.

A town that remembers

In the village below the tower, the lighthouse is more than infrastructure. Children are brought on school trips to see the lens. Weddings are photographed on the headland. When the beam sweeps across the kitchen windows at dusk, people pause for a second without noticing they are doing it.

The town council has voted three times to keep the station staffed, at a cost that its own treasurer calls “irrational and non-negotiable.” A volunteer rota now fills the gaps. There are forty-one names on it, and a waiting list.

The last winter

Yannick is sixty-eight. He has begun to teach, quietly, to a former trawlerman, a retired nurse and a teenager who arrived on a school visit and never really left. They learn the lamp, the logbook, the signals and the weather, and, harder to teach, the habit of attention.

On the last evening of my visit, the wind drops. The lamp comes on by itself, as it always does, and Yannick watches it for a long while without speaking. Then he picks up the logbook, writes the date, and adds a single line: “All well.”
