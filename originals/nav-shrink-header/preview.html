The last good winter

A season of short days, long shadows and the people who still read the sky for a living. Scroll to watch the header settle.

The sky reader

Ines Calloway has been forecasting from a stone hut above Pennick Bay for thirty-one winters. Her instruments are a brass barometer, a notebook and a habit of stepping outside to smell the air before she writes a word.

She says the light is the first thing to change. A week before the snow arrives the horizon turns the colour of a worn coin, and the gulls stop arguing. The notebook, she admits, is only a way of pretending she is not guessing.

“The weather doesn’t lie. We just ask it the wrong questions.”

An essay on short days

There is a particular kind of tiredness that arrives at four in the afternoon in December, when the lamps come on and the day has barely begun to be used. It is not quite sadness. It is closer to the feeling of a held breath.

The old almanacs prescribed soup, early bedtimes and the rereading of familiar books. They were right about all three, and wrong only about how long it would last.

Field notes from the coast road

Frost on the cattle grid, a heron standing in the flooded lane, the café at Lantern Cove open for exactly four hours on Thursdays. We drove the whole road in a day and wrote down everything that did not move.

Bring a flask. Bring gloves with fingers you can undo. Bring a second pair of socks for the person you meet who forgot theirs.

From the archive
