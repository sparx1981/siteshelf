The Last Lamplighter of Saltgate

Every evening at dusk, one man walks the quay with a pole and a key, and the town quietly agrees not to ask why.

By Dara Okoye · Illustration by the Review studio · 12 October

1 A lamplighter's round in Saltgate takes 41 minutes and 112 steps.

Each evening, a little before the last ferry, Tomas Reyes sets down his tea and lifts the pole from its hook by the door. It is seven feet of ash with a brass hook at one end and a small flame at the other, and he has carried it along the quay for thirty-one years.1

The lamps were converted to electricity in 1974, then turned back by a town meeting in 1988, after a winter in which visitors complained that the harbour looked like a car park. Nine of the original twenty-two were restored. They burn gas from a cylinder behind the chandler’s shop, and they need to be lit by hand.

The gas company stopped sending a lamplighter long ago. Reyes simply never stopped. He was meant to retire at 65, a date marked on a card that sat on the town clerk’s desk for three years until somebody put a plant on top of it. “I asked what would happen to the lamps,” he says. “Nobody had an answer, so I kept the key.”

His route is the same every evening. He begins at the chandler’s, walks the quay to the lifeboat steps, turns, and comes back along the water side, touching each lamp with the flame and listening for the soft pop of the mantle catching. There are 112 steps. On a good night he does it in 41 minutes. On a wet one he does it in 50 and says nothing about it.

A question from the school bus

Last spring a schoolchild on the 4:10 bus asked him why he bothered when a switch would do. He thought about it for the length of one lamp. “Because people look,” he said. “They look out of the window while they do the washing up. If the lamp is lit, they know somebody is out here.”

I do not light the street for the people who walk it. I light it for the ones who look out of their windows.

Tomas Reyes

The town has tried, in its way, to make him unnecessary. A trust was formed in 2019 to fund automatic ignition. It raised a good deal of money, held a tasteful launch and then, at a second meeting, voted to spend the money on a new pole and a spare key instead. The minutes record that the motion passed “with great fondness”.

Reyes is quiet about what he gets from it. He keeps a notebook in which he writes the date, the weather and the number of lamps that needed a second try. There are more than eleven thousand entries. Near the back, in the same careful hand, is a line he does not remember writing: Nobody ever thanked the lamps, which is why I do.

When the last lamp is lit, he stands for a moment at the lifeboat steps with his back to the water. The windows along the front are yellow squares. Somewhere a curtain moves, and he lifts the pole a few inches, the way you might raise a hand to a neighbour, then walks home.
